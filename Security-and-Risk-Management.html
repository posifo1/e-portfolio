Unit 1: An Introduction to Security and Risk Management

Learning Outcome

Through this learning activity, I critically examined a range of definitions of Security and Risk Management, developing a comprehensive understanding of how differing interpretations influence organisational policy development, governance, and strategic decision‑making. This review enhanced my ability to evaluate theoretical perspectives and apply them to contemporary security challenges.

I explored core concepts, including assets, threats, vulnerabilities, and controls, and the interrelationships between them. This analysis deepened my insight into how these elements collectively contribute to an organisation’s security posture and overall risk resilience.

Furthermore, I investigated the Risk Management Process—risk identification, assessment, mitigation, monitoring, and review—appreciating how systematic, evidence‑based approaches underpin effective decision‑making while aligning with organisational objectives, legal requirements, and industry standards.

As part of the discussion topic, I engaged with the case study by Kovaitė and Stankevičienė (2019), applying theory in context. I examined their use of the term Industry 4.0, noting examples such as manufacturing automation and cyber‑physical systems. I identified real‑world risks aligned to their categories—e.g., data breaches in interconnected supply chains and operational disruption from system failures. Additionally, I analysed a supporting or contradictory peer‑reviewed article, encouraging critical reflection on differing academic perspectives surrounding Industry 4.0 and its risks.

Overall, this experience strengthened my theoretical understanding and critical analysis skills, reinforcing links between security and risk frameworks and their application to real‑world, technology‑driven contexts.

Unit 3: Introduction to Threat Modelling and Management

Learning Outcomes

Exploring threat modelling and management highlighted how organisations can systematically identify and mitigate security risks. A key insight was distinguishing threats (factors that can exploit weaknesses) from vulnerabilities (system weaknesses). Different frameworks serve distinct purposes: STRIDE categorises threats, DREAD prioritises by impact, Attack Trees visualise attack paths, and PASTA offers a process‑oriented approach aligned with business objectives. Guides and cookbooks provide structured methodologies that connect theory to action. Overall, effective threat modelling is collaborative, strategic, and continuous—best integrated early in system design to build resilience.

I learned to evaluate the effectiveness of these publicly available tools in identifying and analysing potential threats and to create practical threat models based on given scenarios. This process enhanced my understanding of how to apply theoretical concepts to real-world contexts, strengthening my critical thinking and technical skills in threat modelling and management. This unit also included a case study review of Jbair et al. (2022), which explored the application of threat modelling in cyber-physical systems (CPS)—particularly within the energy sector. This case study emphasised the complexity of CPS environments, where digital and physical components are interdependent and must be modelled comprehensively to achieve accurate risk analysis. The accompanying workshop questions deepened my understanding of how to apply threat modelling concepts to real-world CPS scenarios. I learned that identifying key elements and interdependencies in a cyber-physical system is crucial for building a complete and reliable threat model. This understanding supports accurate risk assessment and enhances decision-making. Furthermore, I reflected on how threat modelling can reveal potential attack entry points and vulnerabilities within CPS-based energy systems, while recognizing the challenges associated with system complexity and data integration. Finally, the discussion on scenario-specific metrics and risk assessment methodologies reinforced how prioritizing vulnerabilities through measurable indicators can guide the development of targeted and effective security countermeasures. Overall, the study case and workshop exercises strengthened my ability to apply structured threat modelling approaches to complex, real-world systems, enhancing both analytical precision and strategic thinking in cybersecurity management.

Unit 7: Introduction to Quantitative Risk Modelling

Learning Outcomes

    In consolidating this week’s material, I now view risk modelling through two complementary lenses. The first is qualitative modelling, which structures expert judgement (e.g., interviews, workshops, risk matrices, bow-tie analyses) to surface scenarios, impacts, and controls. The second is quantitative risk modelling (QRM), which expresses uncertainty with numbers—probabilities, loss distributions, and expected values—to compare options and optimise treatment. Within QRM, the major families I engaged with were multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM), Monte Carlo simulation, and Bayesian approaches. MCDM methods (such as AHP or TOPSIS) rank alternatives against weighted criteria when outcomes are multi-dimensional (e.g., security, cost, usability, compliance). Monte Carlo simulation propagates uncertainty by sampling from input distributions to generate a loss or performance distribution; it is well-suited to estimating annualised loss exposure and testing the effect of controls. Bayesian approaches model conditional dependencies and update beliefs with new evidence (e.g., Bayesian networks for attack paths, or posterior risk after deploying a control). Choosing among them depends on the problem structure and data. I would use qualitative modelling to frame threats, assumptions, and stakeholder risk appetite, then pivot to MCDM when trade-offs span incommensurate criteria and we need transparent weighting. Monte Carlo suits problems with measurable variability (frequency, magnitude) and when decision-makers need a full distribution, not a point estimate. Bayesian methods are preferred when prior knowledge is strong, evidence arrives sequentially, or dependencies between events are material. QRM’s core is representing uncertainty explicitly and testing decisions under that uncertainty. Principles include clarity of scope and metrics, calibrated inputs (or elicited priors), validation against reality, and sensitivity analysis. Antipatterns include false precision from coarse risk matrices, arbitrary weights in MCDM, assuming convenient distributions or independence in Monte Carlo, and unexamined or double-counted priors in Bayesian models. Finally, standards anchor these practices in governance. Frameworks such as ISO 31000, ISO/IEC 27001/27005 and NIST RMF provide a common language for risk context, criteria, and treatment plans, and ensure that model outputs flow into policy, control selection, assurance, and continuous improvement. In practice, I would align modelling choices to the standard’s process steps, document assumptions and evidence, and trace results to risk registers and control objectives, ensuring rigour without losing organisational relevance.
