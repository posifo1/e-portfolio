Security and Risk Management

This e-portfolio documents my learning journey in Security and Risk Management. In this module, we will look at the various areas where security and risk overlap and interact with each other. We will review the various methods of risk assessment, looking at both qualitative and quantitative methods. We will discuss several more traditional risk models (such as STRIDE, DREAD and Attack Trees) as well as reviewing the increasing trend towards hybrid models. We will also review common security standards such as PCI-DSS and look at business continuity and disaster recovery solutions. Each unit includes the module title, learning outcomes, and activities/assignments.

Unit 1: An Introduction to Security and Risk Management

Learning Outcome Reflection

Through this learning activity, I critically examined a range of definitions of Security and Risk Management, developing a comprehensive understanding of how differing interpretations influence organisational policy development, governance, and strategic decision-making. This review enhanced my ability to evaluate theoretical perspectives and apply them to contemporary security challenges.

I explored several core concepts within Security and Risk Management, including assets, threats, vulnerabilities, controls and the interrelationships between them. This analysis deepened my insight into how these elements collectively contribute to the creation and maintenance of an organisation’s security posture and overall risk resilience.

Furthermore, I investigated the Risk Management Process, including the key stages of risk identification, assessment, mitigation, monitoring, and review. This allowed me to appreciate how systematic and evidence-based approaches underpin effective decision-making and ensure alignment with organisational objectives, legal requirements, and industry standards.

As part of the discussion topic, I engaged with the case study by Kovaitė and Stankevičienė (2019), which provided a practical lens through which to apply theoretical knowledge. In this task, I examined the authors’ use of the term Industry 4.0, identifying examples such as automation in manufacturing and the integration of cyber-physical systems. I also identified real-world risks aligning with the authors’ risk categories, such as data breaches in interconnected supply chains and operational disruptions caused by system failures. Additionally, I located and analysed a supporting or A contradictory peer-reviewed journal article, which encouraged critical reflection on differing academic perspectives regarding Industry 4.0 and its associated risks.

Overall, this learning experience enhanced my theoretical understanding and critical analysis skills, reinforcing the connection between security and risk management frameworks and their application in real-world, technology-driven contexts.

Unit 3: Introduction to Threat Modelling and Management

Learning Outcomes

The exploration of threat modelling and management provided valuable insights into how organisations can systematically identify and mitigate potential security risks. Understanding the difference between threats and vulnerabilities was a key takeaway, as it clarified that while vulnerabilities are weaknesses within a system, threats are external or internal factors that can exploit those weaknesses. This distinction forms the foundation for practical risk assessment and response strategies. Learning about different threat modelling frameworks, such as STRIDE, DREAD, Attack Trees, and PASTA, highlighted how each model serves distinct purposes. STRIDE, for example, is effective for categorising security threats, while DREAD helps in prioritising them based on their potential impact. Attack Trees provide a visual and logical breakdown of how attacks can occur, and PASTA offers a comprehensive, process-oriented approach that aligns with business objectives. These models demonstrated that threat modelling is not a one-size-fits-all process but rather a flexible practice tailored to an organisation’s risk profile and resources. I also found the discussion on guides and cookbooks for threat modelling particularly useful, as they provide structured methodologies and practical tools for applying these frameworks in real-world contexts. This practical aspect connects theory to action, ensuring that organisations can move from identifying risks to implementing protective measures efficiently. Overall, this week reinforced the importance of a proactive and structured approach to cybersecurity. Threat modelling is not just a technical exercise—it requires collaboration, strategic thinking, and continuous improvement. I now appreciate how essential it is for security professionals to integrate these practices early in the system design process to create resilient and secure infrastructures.
